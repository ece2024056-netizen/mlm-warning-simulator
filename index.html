Reality check

No responsible person should treat "get rich quick" as a plan.

When an MLM depends on constant recruitment, the math becomes the warning. The people near the top may look successful because they sit above many purchases, while later entrants run into saturation, expenses, and impossible recruiting targets.

Most people do not win. FTC guidance warns that most MLM participants make little or no money, and some lose money after expenses.
Pyramid schemes are illegal. If rewards mostly come from recruitment instead of real retail sales, that is a major red flag.
Quick money is the bait. A responsible decision starts by asking who pays, who recruits, and who is left holding the cost.

Break Signal

Once the pyramid exceeds the population limit, later levels are mathematically impossible to fill.

People are finite. Recruitment promises are not. The dots keep moving, but the red line is the limit: every level below the top needs a larger crowd than the one before it.

Why It Fails

A recruitment chain does not need many levels before it asks for more people than exist.
level n = r^n
The table separates two problems: population saturation and money concentration. The FTC warns that most people who join MLMs make little or no money, and some lose money; pyramid-style plans become worse when recruitment and inventory purchases drive the payout.

Research Notes

FTC: Most MLM participants make little or no money, and some lose money after expenses.

Model: This page counts startup costs, recurring purchases, and a small meaningful-earner rate so most participants show losses unless assumptions are made very generous.
